Chapter 2 — Solo → AI-Paired → Critique 20 min

Now Pair With an AI

You already worked the exercises yourself on Pinehurst Realty Group's ListBot. Now a third scenario, Thistlewood Veterinary Group (a fictional multi-vet clinic) and its intake agent TriageBot, gets diagnosed a second way: by prompting an AI assistant, and reviewing what it hands back with the same skepticism you'd bring to a coworker's write-up before it ships to the clinic's lead vet.

Step 1: Diagnose it yourself, first

TriageBot has three tools: get_symptom_report(pet_id), get_vitals(pet_id), and check_recent_visits(pet_id). Thistlewood's lead vet reports three real, observed problems:

  1. For every routine annual wellness visit (a task whose steps never depend on each other — check vitals, check recent visit history, done), TriageBot asks the model to decide the next step fresh, one tool at a time, needing 3 separate reasoning calls for a sequence that's identical every single time.
  2. For an urgent symptom intake (where the correct second check genuinely depends on the first symptom report — a limping dog needs a different follow-up than a vomiting cat), TriageBot instead uses a plan decided entirely up front: always check vitals second, regardless of what the symptom report said. For one real case, a "sudden lethargy" report should have led to checking recent visits for a prior diagnosis, but the fixed plan checked vitals instead, and vitals came back normal — even though the real answer (a missed follow-up from a visit two weeks earlier) was sitting in the visit history the whole time.
  3. When a fixed wellness-visit plan hit a pet ID typo, TriageBot retried the corrected ID, then retried again with a second guess, then a third, a fourth — nothing capped how many times it was willing to rebuild and re-run the plan.

Before reading further, write your own diagnosis: for each of the three problems, name the concept it's really about (using this chapter's vocabulary: wrong planning mode, wrong-plan failure, or a missing planning guard) and propose one concrete fix. Use a notes app or scratch file — there's no grading here, just your own reasoning to compare against Step 3.

Step 2: Ask an AI assistant the same question

Thought process: the prompt needs to hand over all three problems with their real, specific evidence — not a vague "why is my agent's planning bad" — or the AI has nothing concrete to diagnose and will produce generic advice instead of a checkable answer.

Write the prompt you'd give an AI assistant:

Paste your prompt into Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, or whatever assistant you use, and compare what it builds against this page's own critique in Step 3.
Show a strong version of this prompt 
I have an agent called TriageBot with three tools: get_symptom_report(pet_id), get_vitals(pet_id), check_recent_visits(pet_id). Here are three real, observed problems: [paste the three numbered problems above verbatim]. For each problem, name which planning concept it's really about (wrong planning mode for the task's shape, a wrong-plan failure, or a missing planning guard) and propose one concrete, specific code-level fix for each -- not a general "plan better" suggestion.

Step 3: Critique what came back

A plausible-but-flawed AI answer to this exact prompt tends to make three specific mistakes worth watching for:

Common AI diagnosis mistakes on this scenario
  • Mislabeling Problem 1 as "the model is being inefficient." The real issue is a task/strategy mismatch: the wellness-visit steps never depend on each other, so this task should use a fixed plan (Section 4 of the lesson) instead of paying for 3 reasoning calls on a sequence that never varies. A shallow answer says "reduce calls" without naming that the fix is switching planning mode, not tuning the existing one.
  • Treating Problem 2 as a wrong-tool-choice or wrong-argument failure. Every tool call TriageBot made actually succeeded — vitals came back real data. The real failure is that the plan itself locked in the wrong second step before the symptom report was read, exactly the wrong-plan failure class from Section 11 of the lesson. A generic answer often proposes "improve the vitals tool" instead of the real fix: this task needs emergent planning, since the correct second check genuinely depends on the first result.
  • Proposing unlimited retries for Problem 3, or a very high retry count, instead of a hard max_replans cap. "Let it keep trying with better guesses" without a hard, small bound is a subtler version of the exact unbounded-loop problem Chapter 1's max_iterations guard exists to prevent — just one layer up, at the plan level instead of the tool-call level.

Compare the AI's actual answer against your own Step 1 notes and the three watch-outs above. Which one(s) did it get right? Which, if any, did it get wrong the way described here? That comparison — not the AI's fluency — is the real test.
